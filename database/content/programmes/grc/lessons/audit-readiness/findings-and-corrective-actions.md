## Anatomy of a well-written finding

Auditors and GRC teams express findings in a standard structure so that readers can judge them quickly and act on them.

| Element | Question it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Condition | What did we find? | 3 of 25 sampled joiners received core banking access before their line manager approved it. |
| Criteria | What was required? | Access Control Standard §2.3 requires documented approval before access is granted. |
| Cause | Why did it happen? | Urgent requests are handled by phone; the ticket is raised afterwards. |
| Effect | What is the risk? | Staff may hold access that was never authorised, increasing fraud and data exposure risk. |
| Recommendation | What should change? | Enforce approval in the ticketing workflow before provisioning; define an emergency path with retrospective approval within 24 hours. |

Each finding receives a **rating** (for example high, medium, low) based on the effect and how widespread it is. A finding without a cause usually leads to a superficial fix.

## Root cause analysis

Ask "why?" until you reach something the organisation controls and can change. For the example above: *Why was access granted early?* Phone requests. *Why phone?* New staff start before managers raise tickets. *Why?* HR does not notify IT until the start day. The root cause is the HR-to-IT handover, so fixing only IT's process will not stop recurrence.

## Correction versus corrective action

- **Correction**: fix the instance. Review the three accounts and confirm or remove access.
- **Corrective action**: remove the cause. Change the HR onboarding workflow to trigger the access request two weeks before the start date.

Both are usually needed.

## The corrective action plan

| Finding | Action | Owner | Due | Status | Verified by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F-03 early access | Workflow enforces approval before provisioning | IT manager | 31 Jan | In progress | Internal audit |

Good practice:

- **Management response**: the owner agrees (or disputes, with reasons) and commits to a plan.
- **Realistic dates**: missing dates repeatedly is itself a governance signal.
- **Verification**: closure requires evidence that the action was taken *and* is effective, typically by re-testing a new sample.
- **Escalation**: overdue high-rated actions go to executive management and the board committee.

## Managing evidence between audits

Keep an **evidence repository** organised by control, with the period each item covers, who produced it and when. Automate where possible: scheduled exports of MFA coverage, access review records generated by the identity system, backup reports emailed to a monitored mailbox. Some organisations use continuous control monitoring to test key controls automatically every day, turning audit preparation into a routine report rather than a project.

## Repeat findings

A finding that reappears year after year tells the board that corrective action is not working. Track repeat findings explicitly and report them separately; they are often the most useful governance metric an audit produces.
