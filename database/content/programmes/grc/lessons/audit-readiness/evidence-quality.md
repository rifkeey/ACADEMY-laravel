## Audit readiness is continuous

Organisations that scramble for evidence in the week before an audit usually find gaps they cannot close in time. Audit readiness means operating controls so that evidence is produced as a by-product of normal work.

## Planning an audit

An audit plan defines the **objective** (certification, regulatory, internal assurance), the **scope** (controls, systems, period), the **criteria** (which standard or policy), the **approach** (interviews, inspection, sampling, re-performance) and the **schedule**.

## Qualities of good evidence

| Quality | Question | Weak example | Strong example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Relevant | Does it relate to this control? | A generic security policy | The MFA enforcement setting and its scope |
| Reliable | Is the source trustworthy? | A screenshot with no date | A system-generated report with timestamp |
| Sufficient | Does it cover the period? | One example from March | A sample across the whole audit period |
| Timely | Is it current? | A 2023 access review | The access review for this quarter |
| Verifiable | Could an auditor reproduce it? | "We check this regularly" | Ticket history showing each review |

## Testing controls

- **Design effectiveness**: would the control, as designed, address the risk?
- **Operating effectiveness**: did it actually operate as designed throughout the period? This usually needs sampling, e.g. 25 of 300 joiners checked for approved access requests.

## Findings and corrective actions

A finding should state the **condition** (what was found), **criteria** (what was required), **cause**, **effect** (risk) and **recommendation**. Each finding gets an owner, an agreed corrective action and a due date. Track actions to closure and verify they work; re-raising the same finding next year signals a governance problem.
