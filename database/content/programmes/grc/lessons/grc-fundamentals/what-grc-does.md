## Three disciplines, one purpose

**Governance** sets direction: who decides what level of security risk the organisation will accept, who is accountable, and how leadership oversees security. **Risk management** identifies, assesses and treats risks so they stay within that appetite. **Compliance** demonstrates that the organisation meets its obligations: laws, regulations, contracts and its own policies.

They depend on each other. Compliance without risk management produces paperwork that does not reduce harm. Risk management without governance has no authority to make anyone act.

## The document hierarchy

| Document | Purpose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy | States intent and principles; approved by senior management | "All access to customer data requires multi-factor authentication." |
| Standard | Mandatory, specific requirements that implement a policy | "MFA must use an authenticator app or hardware key; SMS is not permitted for administrators." |
| Procedure | Step-by-step instructions | "How to enrol a new staff member in MFA." |
| Guideline | Recommended, non-mandatory good practice | "Tips for choosing a strong passphrase." |

Keep policies short and stable. Put detail that changes often into standards and procedures, so the board does not need to re-approve every technical update.

## Accountability and oversight

A healthy structure separates the people who **own** risks (business leaders), those who **advise and challenge** (security and risk functions) and those who provide **independent assurance** (internal audit). This is often described as the "three lines" model. Oversight usually sits with the board or a board risk committee, informed by regular, honest reporting.

NIST CSF 2.0 added a dedicated **Govern** Function precisely because these responsibilities underpin every other security outcome.
